Table for One

The rise of solo dining and how the people we eat with change throughout our lives.

By Your Name

Who will you be sharing dinner with tonight? Maybe you've finally worked up the courage to ask your crush out on a date. Or perhaps your evening looks more like a picture-perfect family meal, with smiling children happily finishing their vegetables. Then again, you might simply be sitting down to eat on your own.

Before you joke online about being #ForeverAlone, it's worth knowing that dining alone has become increasingly common over the years.

Back in 2003, the American Time Use Survey found that people ate 32% of their meals alone. As the survey continued over the following years, that figure gradually climbed, reaching 35% by 2015.

A small percentage increase might not seem dramatic, but over the course of a month it translates into an additional day spent eating alone. Even more importantly, that increase isn't experienced equally by everyone.

OVERVIEW

Frequency of dining alone by age

Generally speaking, as we get older, we eat alone more and more frequently. But there are a few interesting things worth noting here.

30-SOMETHING DIP

First, look at the early thirties

First, look at this dip for people in their 30’s. These folks were eating noticeably fewer meals alone per month than people just a decade younger than them.

70-SOMETHING SOLO DINERS

A steep upward trend

I also noticed that once people hit 70 years old, they experienced a steep upward trend in solitary eating. Though this data is from 2003 and a lot has changed by 2015.

2015 VS 2003

Two years of data together

Once I added the data from 2015, the patterns really caught my attention. It looks like millennials and baby-boomers finally have something in common. Both groups have begun eating alone more frequently than in 2003.

AGE GROUPS

The full age-group story

I decided to slice the population into five age groups: 15 - 19, 20 - 30, 30 - 50, 50 - 75, and 75 - 85. These curves seem to follow family life: living with parents, moving out, starting a family, having kids, empty nests, and growing old together.

The people around our table change over time.

Childhood dinners are usually shared with parents and siblings. As we grow older, friends become our regular companions before partners and children gradually take their place. Eventually, many people find themselves eating alone more often than they once imagined.

Every meal tells a story.

The people sitting across from us are constantly changing throughout our lives. Families grow, friendships evolve, children leave home, and routines shift. Looking at decades of meals together reveals not just eating habits, but the changing shape of our relationships.
